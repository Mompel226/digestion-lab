The three modes

  • Mastery (the normal one). Check as often as you like. You are told which parts are wrong, but never the answer — you have to work it out. Keep going until every question is right, then Hand in.

Switching mode keeps a separate record for each, so trying a Test does not wipe your Mastery progress.

How to use it

  • Click any organ on the body, or any step on the bar along the bottom.
  • Each station has two tabs: Learn — what you must know, with the diagrams and the real photographs from the lesson set beside the sentence each one illustrates — and Practise, the questions.
  • The body follows you. Open a station and the camera on the left flies to the organ; as it arrives, a professional illustration of that organ appears on the body itself, in place, so you can see where it sits and what it looks like inside. ⤢ Whole body pulls the camera back.
  • Follow the food sends a meal along the whole canal and opens each station as it arrives.
  • Your work is saved in this browser on this device. Use the same computer each time, and do not clear your browsing data before you have handed in.

What the colours mean

  • The coloured chips at the top of a station show which of the five processes happen there — ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation, egestion.
  • A green-bordered box, after the facts, is the mistake students most often make at that station — read the facts first, then test yourself against it.
  • A small S badge on a sentence means Supplement: examined on Paper 4 (Extended) only. An extension badge means it is taught for understanding but is not in the 2026–28 syllabus.
  • A blue box marked “Real science” is true but not examined — read it only once the exam answer is secure.
  • In Learn, the five processes each wear a numbered chip — 1 ingestion, 2 digestion, 3 absorption, 4 assimilation, 5 egestion — so you can see both which stage a sentence is about and where it comes in the order. Physical and chemical digestion are both stage 2, told apart by colour.
  • A key word in the same colour as a chip belongs to that stage. Food molecules are the one exception: they are marked with a dotted underline, because you must never mix them up with the enzymes acting on them.
  • Words that are deliberately not coloured are worth noticing too — excretion stays plain black, because it is not one of the five processes.
  • The colour key sits at the top of every Learn tab, so you never have to remember what a colour means.
  • Highlighted words can be clicked. A word with a dotted underline and a 🔍 opens a small picture right there — click fats and oils to see what they actually are. A word with a solid underline and an ↗ takes you to the station that explains it — click peristalsis anywhere and it scrolls you to the exact sentence that explains it and flashes it, with a “back” button so you do not lose your place.
  • Under the key words you will find “Where this comes back later in the course” — the same organ turning up in a later topic, with the syllabus reference. You do not need it yet; it is there so the course joins up.
  • A station badged “Beyond the syllabus” (the epiglottis) is worth knowing but is not named in 0610. Turn those off with the “Beyond syllabus” button.

What is simplified here

  • The body is a real anatomical plate, so the organs are in their true positions. The liver is on your left as you look at the screen, which is the patient's right — that is the standard convention, and it is how the diagram will be drawn in the exam.
  • The gall bladder station lights up the gall bladder and the bile ducts running through the liver, because they are drawn as one piece. That is deliberate: it shows the whole route bile takes — liver → ducts → gall bladder → bile duct → duodenum.
  • Optimum pH values are given as single figures (pepsin ≈ 2, amylase ≈ 7, trypsin and lipase ≈ 8). Real enzymes work across a range; the figure is the peak.
  • The food particle shrinks and changes colour as it travels. That is a visual aid, not a measurement.
  • The small intestine is drawn as a few tidy coils. In life it is 6–7 m of tightly packed loops.
