What the colours mean
  • The five processes each wear a numbered chip — 1 ingestion, 2 digestion, 3 absorption, 4 assimilation, 5 egestion — so you can see both which stage a sentence is about and where it comes in the order. Physical and chemical digestion are both stage 2, told apart by colour.
  • A key word in the same colour as a chip belongs to that stage. Food molecules are the exception: dotted underline, because you must never mix them up with the enzymes acting on them.
  • Words deliberately not coloured are worth noticing — excretion stays plain black, because it is not one of the five.
  • The colour key sits at the top of every Learn tab, so you never have to remember.
  • A green-bordered box is the mistake students most often make there. A small S badge means Supplement (Paper 4 only). A blue “Real science” box is true but not examined. “Beyond the syllabus” is worth knowing but not named in 0610 — turn those off with the button.
  • Under the key words, “Where this comes back later in the course” shows the same organ turning up in a later topic, with the syllabus reference.
What is simplified here
  • The body is a real anatomical plate, so the organs are in their true positions. The liver is on your left as you look at the screen — the patient's right. That is the convention, and how the exam will draw it.
  • The gall bladder station lights the gall bladder and the bile ducts in the liver: they are drawn as one piece, which shows the whole route — liver → ducts → gall bladder → bile duct → duodenum.
  • Optimum pH values are single figures (pepsin ≈ 2, amylase ≈ 7, trypsin and lipase ≈ 8). Real enzymes work across a range; the figure is the peak.
  • The food particle shrinks and changes colour as it travels: a visual aid, not a measurement.
  • The small intestine is drawn as a few tidy coils. In life it is 6–7 m of tightly packed loops.
